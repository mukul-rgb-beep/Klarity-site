Mac Storage Full After a macOS Update? Where the Space Went

You updated overnight and this morning there are thirty fewer gigabytes and a grey bar that explains nothing. The space is still there, in folders with names. Almost nobody finds it, because macOS hands you a bar instead of a map.

Sixty gigabytes on Monday. Twelve on Tuesday.

You installed the update because macOS asked and it was late. This morning a document will not save, and System Settings → General → Storage shows a coloured bar with a large grey block labelled System Data that you cannot click into.

Nothing has gone wrong. An update replaces a huge number of files at once, and the leftovers land in a handful of specific folders that all have names. The space is findable. Most people never find it because the tool macOS gives them for looking is a bar chart with seven categories and no way to open any of them.

That is the actual problem — not a shortage of advice, a shortage of sight. There is a way to see the whole disk in about five minutes, and the rest of this post is what you will be looking at.

A bar tells you the shape of the problem, not where it is

Storage settings can say that 90 GB is System Data. It cannot say that 38 GB of that is old iPhone backups and 14 GB an installer you no longer need. So the hunt becomes hunches checked one folder at a time with Cmd + I, and it often ends without an answer, because the folder that grew is the one you never thought to check.

The question worth answering is not what is generally safe to delete on a Mac. It is what on this Mac got big — and that needs a picture of your disk.

The fast path: scan, look at the biggest rectangles, act on them

Klarity, the Mac disk space analyzer I build, draws your disk as a treemap. Every folder is a rectangle and its area is the space it holds, so the folder that swallowed your free space is the largest shape on screen. You do not deduce which one it is. You look at it.

Each rectangle is also coloured by when it last changed, which is the distinction that matters after an update: big and old is yours to deal with; big and changed last night is usually something macOS is still using.

Then deal with it in the same window: right-click the rectangle and Move to Trash. It asks first every time, a folder of 1 GB or more needs a deliberate slide to confirm rather than a click, and it only ever moves things to the Trash — never deletes outright, never empties the Trash for you. There is an icicle view for reading the hierarchy as layers instead of areas, and a large-file finder for single enormous files. Since version 1.3 it asks for disk access once per volume and remembers it, so trashing works anywhere you have scanned (see the changelog).

Where the space went: what the big rectangles turn out to be

After an update the same few things show up large. Here is how to recognise each on your own screen.

  • A 10 GB-plus block in Applications called Install macOS <name>.app. The full installer, still there after doing its job. Move it to the Trash and empty the Trash; it is re-downloadable from the App Store. The easiest win on the list. (Clearing room before an upgrade instead? See how much free space to clear before upgrading.)
  • A large folder under Application Support named MobileSync. iPhone and iPad backups, one folder each, commonly 10 to 50 GB. Delete them the supported way rather than by hand: Finder → your device in the sidebar → Manage Backups.
  • A Developer folder that dwarfs everything else. Only if you write software. Xcode/iOS DeviceSupport keeps a folder per OS version you have debugged against, and DerivedData is build output that regenerates itself. Both can go. Remove unused simulator runtimes through Xcode → Settings → Platforms.
  • A large Caches folder coloured as changed today. Browsers, Mail and the search index all rebuild after an update, so it climbs for a day or two and then levels off. The colour is the tell: clearing it mid-rebuild makes your Mac do the work twice.
  • The Trash. Things in it still occupy the disk. Obvious, and still how a fair number of these searches end.

If the biggest thing on your scan is something you do not recognise, what System Data on a Mac actually is covers what lives in there and what is safe to remove.

What comes back on its own after a macOS update

Some of the missing space is not yours to reclaim. An update keeps snapshots of the system as it was before the install, and stages the update payload on disk while it works. macOS releases both by itself, usually within a day or two of a restart — Apple documents the same behaviour in its own guide to a full startup disk — and Spotlight is reindexing meanwhile, so the Storage settings numbers are not fully trustworthy until it finishes.

Restart, give it a day, and expect a chunk to come back with no action from you. Do not switch off Time Machine to stop the number growing — that trades a temporary space problem for a permanent recovery problem.

One limitation, before you pay for anything

Being straight about this, because it affects this exact scenario: the shipping version of Klarity skips hidden folders, and macOS marks ~/Library as hidden. A plain Home scan therefore misses Caches, Developer and Containers — unhelpful, given that ~/Library is where much post-update growth lives.

The workaround is one step: choose Choose Folder…, press Cmd + Shift + G in the panel that opens, and type ~/Library. Scanning a folder as the root of its own scan works whether it is hidden or not. Hidden-folder scanning and a Library shortcut ship in 1.3.1.

Questions people ask

Why is my Mac storage full after updating macOS?

An update rewrites an enormous number of files at once, and things pile up around it: snapshots of the system as it was before the install, the installer app left in Applications, caches rebuilding themselves, and often a phone backup taken the same week. Thirty gigabytes disappearing overnight is ordinary. The trouble is that Storage settings will not tell you which of those it was.

Will the space come back on its own after a macOS update?

Some of it. macOS releases the update snapshots and the staged update payload itself, usually within a day or two of a restart, and the Storage settings numbers only settle once Spotlight has finished reindexing. What never comes back on its own is the installer app, old device backups and developer tooling.

Is it safe to delete the Install macOS app after updating?

Yes. Once your Mac is running the new version, the installer sitting in Applications has done its job and is more than ten gigabytes of dead weight. Move it to the Trash and empty the Trash. You can download it again from the App Store if you ever need it.

How do I find what is actually using the space, rather than guessing?

Scan the disk with a treemap. It draws every folder as a shape whose area is the space it holds, so the folders responsible are the largest ones on screen and you read the answer instead of working it out. That is what Klarity does, for $6.99 once on the Mac App Store.

Does Klarity delete my files?

No. It only ever moves things to the Trash, and it asks first every time. A folder of 1 GB or more needs a deliberate slide to confirm rather than a click, and anything smaller gets a plain confirmation alert. It never deletes outright and never empties the Trash for you.
