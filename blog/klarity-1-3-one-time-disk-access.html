Klarity 1.3: Why a Sandboxed Mac App Cannot Delete Your Files

Version 1.3 shipped today, and its headline feature is that Move to Trash works. That sounds like an admission, and it is. Here is what Apple’s sandbox actually does, and why fixing it took a whole release.

The feature that only worked sometimes

Klarity Disk 1.3, build 1300, went live on the Mac App Store today. The change worth writing about is not a new chart. It is that right-clicking a folder and choosing Move to Trash now works where you would expect it to, rather than where I happened to have tested.

Before 1.3 the behaviour depended on where the file was, in a way I could explain and no user should have to. Inside a folder you had already pointed Klarity at: fine. In Downloads, before any such grant: nothing, or an error after the fact. On an external drive: also nothing. The app was not broken in the ordinary sense — it was doing exactly what macOS allowed, which was less than the interface implied. That gap is the subject of this post.

What the sandbox actually is

Every app on the Mac App Store runs in Apple’s app sandbox. It is not a setting, it is a condition of being on the store. At launch the kernel wraps the app in a policy that says, roughly: read and write inside your own container directory and nowhere else, unless you have been given a specific exception.

Those exceptions are called entitlements. Klarity declares exactly one for files, user-selected.read-write, and nothing else: no network, no camera, no microphone, no contacts. It cannot make a network connection at all, which is the other thing I care about and have written about before.

user-selected.read-write is narrower than it sounds. It means: the only files this app may touch are ones you have explicitly pointed at, through a system file picker. Not your home folder, not Downloads, not a folder whose path the app happens to know. If Klarity builds a path and calls FileManager on it with no grant behind it, the call fails — and it tends to fail quietly, as an empty directory listing rather than a loud error.

Which is how you get a disk analyzer that reports your Downloads folder as containing nothing at all.

The picker is not Klarity’s picker

The interesting part is how the exception gets made, because almost nobody outside the platform knows the mechanism. When a sandboxed app opens a file picker, the panel you see is not drawn by that app. macOS runs it in a separate system process — the one Apple’s documentation calls the powerbox. The app asks for a panel and gets back a result. It cannot read the names of files you scroll past, and it cannot see what you decided not to choose. The panel is a piece of the system that happens to appear inside the app’s window.

The moment you click Open, the kernel widens the app’s sandbox to include what you chose. That is the whole model in one sentence: your click is the permission. Not a dialog the app can phrase persuasively, not a checkbox buried in settings — an actual selection, made in a panel the app cannot influence.

The access you get that way normally dies when the app quits. To keep it, the app asks macOS for a security-scoped bookmark: an opaque blob encoding both the location and the permission, stored and handed back on the next launch to reopen the same access. That is the piece that turns “you allowed this once” into “you allowed this”.

What 1.3 actually changed

Version 1.2 kept one bookmark. One. You could grant Klarity access to a folder, and that was the folder it had; everything else took the quiet-failure path above. The fix in 1.3 is not clever, just done properly, and it has three parts.

Grants are stored per volume, not per folder. When Klarity needs a path it cannot reach, it works out which volume root that path belongs to — / for your startup disk, /Volumes/<name> for anything external — and asks for that. A store now keeps many such bookmarks and reopens all of them at launch, so the question is asked once per drive, not once per folder and certainly not once per file.

Access is checked before anything is attempted. Every scan and every trash operation now goes through one coordinator whose job is to answer “can we reach this yet, and if not, ask”. If a grant already covers the path it returns at once and you see nothing. If not, you get a single sheet naming the volume and the reason — “Klarity needs this to move ‘DerivedData’ to the Trash” — then the system picker, opened at that volume. No error-then-fix loop, because the check happens before the operation rather than after it fails.

Scanning a folder it cannot read asks, instead of returning nothing. Same coordinator, applied to the scan path. Pointing Klarity at Downloads before any grant used to produce a confident, empty, wrong answer. Now it asks.

Scan Macintosh HD, and stop counting it twice

Once grants work per volume, scanning the whole startup disk stops being a special case, so 1.3 adds a header button that does it in one click. That surfaced a second bug, and a nice example of modern macOS being strange. Since Catalina your startup disk is really two APFS volumes, and the data volume — the one holding everything you own — appears in two places at once: as /System/Volumes/Data, and, through firmlinks, as the familiar /Users and /Applications. Walk / naively and you add the whole disk to the whole disk, reporting a Mac twice as full as it is. 1.3 skips volume roots below the scan root, so the total is the total.

When macOS says no and means it

Some deletions fail even with a perfectly good grant, because the file is owned by root or protected by System Integrity Protection. Nothing an App Store app can do will help there.

The old behaviour was to show a Grant Access prompt, which asked you to perform a ritual that could not possibly succeed. 1.3 tells the two cases apart. A permission failure outside what you have granted still offers Grant Access, which is now rare. One inside a granted area means macOS itself is refusing, so Klarity says “Finder needs an admin password to delete this” and offers Reveal in Finder. Not a solution. But an honest sentence beats a hopeful one.

Smaller things in the same release: Refresh Interval in Settings now actually changes how often RAM and process data update, and is remembered — it was being read once and then ignored, which is embarrassing to write down. Menu bar usage text and display mode persist across launches too. And the build number now shows after the version, e.g. 1.3 (1300), so a bug report can name a build. Full list on the changelog.

Nothing changes about what Klarity does with access

A grant lets Klarity read sizes and move items to the Trash. It never empties the Trash, never deletes outright, and never touches anything without a confirmation — a folder over 1 GB needs a deliberate slide to confirm. And it makes no network connections, so nothing it learns about your disk can leave your Mac even in principle.

What 1.3 does not do

One limitation, stated plainly, because you will hit it and I would rather you heard it here. The shipping scanner passes .skipsHiddenFiles, and macOS marks ~/Library as hidden. So a plain scan of your Home folder does not include ~/Library — precisely where caches, Xcode DerivedData, simulator runtimes and app containers live. The folder that explains most of the grey “System Data” bar is the one a Home scan silently leaves out, and your Home total therefore looks smaller than it should. That is not the sandbox’s fault; it is mine.

Hidden-folder scanning, plus a Library shortcut in the folder menu, is what 1.3.1 is for. Until it ships the workaround is one step: scan ~/Library as the root of its own scan, via Choose Folder… and then Cmd + Shift + G in the open panel. Scanning a folder as the root works whether it is hidden or not. The walkthrough, and what is safe to delete once you are in there, is in what System Data on a Mac actually is.

Why bother explaining any of this

I could have shipped 1.3 with a line saying “improved reliability” and moved on. But a Mac disk space analyzer is an app you point at your entire life’s files and then let move things to the Trash. The reasonable response to that is suspicion, and suspicion is answered with specifics, not reassurance.

The sandbox is why the app cannot see your disk until you show it. The powerbox is why the picker is worth trusting. The bookmark is why you are asked once and not every launch. What 1.3 fixed is that Klarity had been hiding all of that behind a button that sometimes did nothing.

If it still does nothing somewhere I have not thought of, tell me — I answer my own support mail. And if you are here for the practical question of what to actually delete, start with how to clean up Mac storage safely, or, if this began right after a system update, where the space goes after a macOS update.
