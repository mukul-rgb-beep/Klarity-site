How Much Mac Storage Do You Need? Measure Before You Buy

Before you buy a new Mac this Black Friday, or a bigger one: how to choose between 256 GB, 512 GB and 1 TB from what is really on the Mac you have, not from guesswork.

Storage is the one choice you cannot change later

Black Friday is a good week to buy a Mac, and a bad week to guess. You can return a colour you dislike. You cannot add storage afterwards: Apple offers no way to add internal storage to a MacBook, iMac or Mac mini after you buy it, so the number you pick at checkout is the number you live with for the next five or six years.

Most people choose by rumour: a friend says 256 GB is plenty, a forum says never less than 1 TB. Neither is about you. The useful answer is already sitting on the Mac you own: how much space you use today, how much of it is still growing, and how much could live somewhere else.

The trouble is that you cannot see it. Storage settings shows a coloured bar with a few broad categories. It cannot tell you that 80 GB of “Documents” is a video project you finished three years ago, or that the folder growing fastest this year is your photo library. That is the gap this guide fills, in three steps: measure, sort, then add up.

Step one: measure the Mac you have, with Klarity

Klarity, the Mac disk space analyzer I build, draws your disk as a treemap. Every folder is a rectangle, and its area is the space it holds. Click Macintosh HD in the sidebar for the whole disk, or Home for just your own files, and give Klarity access once if it asks. It scans hidden folders too, such as your Library, where app data and old iPhone backups live, so nothing large stays out of the picture.

The sizes are the space each item really takes on this disk, the figure Finder calls “on disk”. Files that iCloud has already moved off the Mac count as almost nothing, and a virtual machine counts for what it has actually filled, not the larger size it is allowed to grow to. You are measuring what this Mac holds, which is what your next one would have to hold.

Then look at the colour. By default Klarity colours each rectangle by Age: how long since anything in it last changed. Blue is this month, green this year, then gold for one to two years, orange for two to three, and red for three years or more.

That colour is the whole buying question in one picture. Big and blue is space you are actively using and adding to; it has to fit on the next Mac, with room to grow. Big and red is space you are keeping but not using; it may not need to be on the internal disk at all. The bar along the bottom of the window adds the same thing up for the whole scan: how much of it is from this month, this year, and so on.

Switch the toolbar from Age to Type for the second half of the picture. Files are coloured by kind: video, images, audio, documents, archives and disk images, apps. Folders keep a colour of their own, so Type mode helps most once you double-click into a big folder and want to know whether it is mostly video, photos or old installers.

Step two: sort what you see into three piles

With the scan open, put each of the big rectangles into one of three piles.

Pile one: it stays, and it grows. The blue and green rectangles you open every week. Typical members:

  • Your Photos library, if you keep the full originals on the Mac. It only ever gets bigger, and every new iPhone shoots larger files than the last.
  • Video you are still working on. A few minutes of 4K footage runs to gigabytes, and editing apps keep render files on top.
  • Developer tools. Xcode with its simulators, and containers, can quietly reach tens of gigabytes. On my own Mac the simulators alone take 34 GB.
  • Virtual machines you still start up, and games you still play.
  • Local AI models, if you run them. Each one is often several to tens of gigabytes, and people rarely stop at one.

Pile two: it is yours, but it could live elsewhere. The orange and red rectangles. Finished video projects, an old virtual machine, a music archive, a decade of photo exports, installers and disk images in Downloads. You want to keep them; you do not need them on the internal disk to keep them. An external drive or iCloud can hold this pile.

Pile three: the space macOS takes for itself. You cannot see all of this in a scan, and you cannot shrink it, so plan for it rather than measure it:

  • Apple Intelligence. Apple says its on-device models take up to 14 GB on recent Macs, but owners are measuring 20 to 30 GB, and in macOS 27 it downloads whether you use it or not. Why Apple Intelligence takes so much space has the details.
  • Room for updates. A major macOS upgrade needs free space to unpack itself: macOS 27 wanted about 40 GB free to install, and there will be a macOS 28, 29 and 30 during the life of a Mac you buy now.
  • Working room. Caches, temporary files and local Time Machine snapshots come and go, and a nearly full Mac has nowhere to put them.

Step three: do the arithmetic

Now the sum. It is rough on purpose: the sizes double at each step, so you only need to know which side of the line you are on.

  1. Pile one, today. Add up the big blue and green rectangles, and the smaller things you never think about, from your scan of Macintosh HD.
  2. Pile one, in five years. Look at how much of the scan is from this month and this year in the bar along the bottom. If your photo library or projects grew by 40 GB this year, assume at least that again every year.
  3. Add pile two only if it is staying. If you will not use an external drive or pay for more iCloud, it all comes along.
  4. Add pile three. Allow 60 to 80 GB for macOS itself, Apple Intelligence and update room together. That is a planning figure, not an Apple one, and it errs on the generous side.
  5. Leave headroom. Aim to end up with at least 15 to 20 percent of the disk still free in year five. That is a rule of thumb, not an Apple figure, but it is roughly where a Mac stops warning you.

Take the scan in the screenshot above. Home holds about 294 GB. The red Movies folder (81 GB), the orange Parallels virtual machine (44 GB) and the red Music folder (9 GB) have not changed in years: about 134 GB of pile two. The rest, about 160 GB, is active, led by a 62 GB photo library and a Library folder full of developer tools, both blue. Keep everything on the next Mac and the sum points to 1 TB. Move the old film projects and the virtual machine to an external drive, and 512 GB holds the active part, the system’s share and a few years of growth with room to spare.

What each size suits, roughly

  • 256 GB. Workable if your active pile is well under 100 GB, your photos live in iCloud, and you do not edit video, run virtual machines or write software. You will need to keep an eye on it; living with a 256 GB Mac is a guide to doing that in ten minutes a month. The MacBook Neo still starts at 256 GB; the MacBook Air has started at 512 GB since March.
  • 512 GB. The comfortable middle for most people: a photo library kept on the Mac, some video, a few years of growth, and system updates without a fight.
  • 1 TB or more. For a pile one that is already above 250 GB or growing fast: video editors, photographers who keep full originals, developers, anyone running virtual machines or local AI models, and people who want everything on one disk with no external drive at all.

The cheaper alternative: an external SSD for pile two

Apple charges a premium for each step up, and prices have moved this year. At the time of writing, the MacBook Neo’s only step up, from 256 to 512 GB, adds $100 in the US and also brings Touch ID, after Apple raised Neo prices in June. On most other Macs each step costs more. Check Apple’s configurator for today’s figure in your country before you decide.

A portable SSD of the same size usually costs less than the internal step, and it works with the next Mac too. It suits pile two well: finished projects, archives, old virtual machines, even a Photos library, which Apple lets you move to an external drive. The trade-offs are real, though:

  • It has to be plugged in whenever you want what is on it.
  • It is not a backup. If the only copy of your old projects is on one external drive, losing the drive loses them. Keep a second copy, and give Time Machine its own drive.
  • Pile one belongs inside. If most of your big rectangles are blue, an external drive will not save you from the bigger internal disk; buy the bigger disk.

Questions people ask

Is 256 GB enough for a Mac in 2026?

For some people, yes: if you work mostly in a browser and office apps, keep your photos in iCloud, and do not edit video, run virtual machines or write software. Remember that macOS, Apple Intelligence and room for updates take a real share of a small disk. If what you actively use is already near 100 GB, choose 512 GB.

Should I get 512 GB or 1 TB?

Measure your current Mac first. Add up what you use and add to regularly, estimate five years of growth, and allow for the system and some free space. If that lands under about 400 GB, 512 GB is enough. If it lands above that, or you want old projects and virtual machines on the internal disk too, choose 1 TB.

Is an external SSD a good alternative to more internal storage?

For files you keep but rarely open, yes: finished projects, archives, old virtual machines, even a Photos library. A portable SSD usually costs less than Apple’s storage upgrade and moves to your next Mac. It has to be plugged in to use, and it is not a backup, so keep a second copy of anything important.

How much free space should I keep on a Mac?

Apple does not publish a figure. A common rule of thumb is 15 to 20 percent of the disk, and enough for the next macOS upgrade, which for macOS 27 meant about 40 GB free. A Mac that is nearly full struggles with updates, caches and backups.

Can Klarity tell me how much storage to buy?

Not by itself. Klarity shows what is on your Mac, how big each folder is, and how long since it last changed, so you can see what is growing and what has sat untouched for years. The arithmetic in this guide turns that into a size; the decision is yours.
