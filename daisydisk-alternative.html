DaisyDisk Alternatives for Mac: An Honest List

By Mukul Mehra, the developer of Klarity · Updated 21 November 2026

I write Klarity, one of the apps on this list, so read this page knowing I have a side. What I have tried to do is describe every app here the way I would want my own described: accurately, and with what it does better than mine. DaisyDisk is a genuinely good piece of Mac software, on the Mac App Store since 2011, and a lot of people are perfectly happy with it. If you are one of them, you probably do not need to switch.

The prices, versions and features below come from each developer’s own site and the US Mac App Store, read on 21 November 2026.

The short answer

  • You want it free: GrandPerspective, which draws a treemap, or OmniDiskSweeper, which gives a plain list. Both are free from their makers’ sites.
  • You want nothing new installed: the Storage view in System Settings already lists your largest files.
  • You think in rectangles and want to see what is old: Klarity, $6.99 once. This one is mine.
  • You like the rings and only wondered about the price: stay with DaisyDisk. It is $9.99 once, and it has a free trial on its own site.

GrandPerspective: free, open source, a treemap

GrandPerspective has been released since 2005 and is still updated: version 3.8.1 came out in September 2026. It draws a treemap, where every file is a rectangle as large as the space it takes. It is free from its own site and $2.99 on the Mac App Store, and its developer says you get the same app either way. The code is open source.

What it does better than Klarity: it costs nothing, anyone can read its code, and it has twenty years of releases behind it. It can colour files by type, by folder, or by when they were created, changed or last opened. It can save a scan to look at later and filter what it shows.

What to know: it keeps to that one view. Deleting is switched off until you turn it on in its settings, and then files go to the Trash. It needs macOS 14 or later.

Pick it if free matters most and a treemap suits you. For many people that is the whole answer, and you can stop reading here.

OmniDiskSweeper: free, and a list instead of a chart

OmniDiskSweeper is a free utility from The Omni Group. It has no chart at all. It lists the files and folders on a drive from largest to smallest, and lets you open them or move them to the Trash.

What it does better than Klarity: it is free, and there is nothing to learn. If a chart is noise to you and you only want the biggest things at the top, a sorted list is quicker. Omni says the whole app works with VoiceOver.

What to know: the current version, 1.16, is listed as supported on macOS 11 through macOS 26. In September 2026 Omni wrote that its test builds were updated for macOS 27, so check its page for the build that matches your Mac.

Pick it if you want a free list and no picture.

The Storage view built into macOS

Before installing anything, open the Apple menu, then System Settings, General, Storage. Apple’s storage view shows how much space each kind of file takes. Categories with an Info button open a list where you can select files and delete them, and Documents has a list of large files and a file browser with folder sizes.

What it does better than any app here: it is already on your Mac, it is free, and Apple made it.

What to know: it is categories and lists, not a map of your folders. And of System Data, often the largest mystery, Apple says you cannot manage what is in that category. That is where a map of the disk earns its keep; I wrote about it in what System Data is.

Klarity: a treemap that shows what is old

This is the one I make. Klarity draws your disk as a treemap or an icicle chart and can colour it by how long ago files were last changed, so the large, old things stand out. It has a list of large files, a memory monitor in the menu bar, and no network access at all. It is $6.99 once on the Mac App Store.

What it does not have: a free trial. It also cannot scan cloud drives or scan as an administrator, it needs macOS 14.6 or later, and it is young: the first version came out in February 2026. The sections below set it beside DaisyDisk point by point.

Other names you will see

  • Disk Inventory X was the free treemap for years, and older lists still recommend it. Its last release is from December 2019. Its developer lists it for macOS 10.13 to 10.15 and for Intel processors, and says macOS will complain when you open it because the app is not notarised. On an Apple silicon Mac it could only run through Rosetta, which Apple is keeping only through macOS 27, and I have not tested whether it still opens there. GrandPerspective is the maintained free treemap today.
  • Disk Map is $5.99 on the Mac App Store. It draws a treemap, can colour files by age, and deletes to the Trash or permanently.
  • DiskLens: Disk Space Analyzer is $2.99 on the Mac App Store and new in June 2026. It offers both a treemap and a ring chart and looks for duplicates. It does not delete anything itself, and it needs macOS 26. A second, unrelated app on the store shares the name; that one is a cache cleaner.
  • Space Radar is free and open source, with ring, treemap and flame-graph views. You download it from GitHub, not from the App Store.
  • WhatSize is $14.99 once from its own site, with a 30-day trial. It shows sizes in columns like the Finder’s.

Where DaisyDisk is great

  • It is beautiful, and that matters. The sunburst ring is one of the most recognisable pieces of interface design on the Mac, and people remember it.
  • It has fifteen years of polish behind it. Shipping since 2011 means an enormous number of edge cases have been found and fixed on real disks. A newer app cannot claim that.
  • Its reputation is earned. DaisyDisk is well-regarded for good reasons, and a $9.99 one-time purchase on the Mac App Store is a fair price for what it does. Nobody buying it is being taken advantage of.
  • It reaches further than a sandboxed app can. The edition from DaisyDisk’s own site can scan as an administrator, show what sits in hidden space and clear purgeable space. DaisyDisk can also scan Dropbox, Google Drive, OneDrive and Box. Klarity does none of that.
  • You can try it before paying. The free trial on its site has no time limit; deleting stays locked until you buy. The Mac App Store edition has no trial.

Klarity next to DaisyDisk, point by point

A treemap and an icicle chart instead of a sunburst

This is the real difference, and it is about how you read sizes rather than which app is “better”. A sunburst encodes size as the angle of a wedge; a treemap encodes it as the area of a rectangle. People are measurably better at comparing areas than angles, and in a ring chart the outer levels get thin and hard to hit. Klarity gives you a treemap for spotting the one enormous folder at a glance and an icicle chart for reading the hierarchy top-down; I wrote up the reasoning in treemap and icicle vs sunburst. If you prefer the sunburst, that is a legitimate preference and this page will not talk you out of it.

$6.99 once

Klarity is $6.99 on the Mac App Store, paid once; DaisyDisk is $9.99, also paid once. Three dollars is not a dramatic saving and I would not switch apps over it alone — but neither charges you again next year, which puts both above the cleanup suites that want $30–50 a year for the same job.

No network access, at all

Klarity ships with no network entitlement. The sandbox forbids incoming and outgoing connections, which means it is not a promise in a privacy policy — it is enforced by macOS. There are no accounts, no analytics, and nothing to opt out of. A disk analyser sees the name of every file you own, so I would rather the app simply have no way to send that anywhere.

To be fair to DaisyDisk: its privacy policy says scan data stays on your Mac and nothing is sent out, and its App Store label reads “Data Not Collected”. It does go online when you ask it to scan a cloud drive, and the edition from its own site keeps your licence email on its server.

A RAM and process monitor in the menu bar

Klarity also watches memory: a menu bar readout of RAM pressure and the processes using it, which replaced a second utility I was running purely for that. If you never think about memory, ignore this line.

An age heatmap

Klarity can colour the map by how old files are rather than how big they are. Large and old together is usually the safest thing to delete — the 40 GB of footage untouched since 2023, rather than the 40 GB you are using this week. There is also a large file finder for a flat list of the biggest items. GrandPerspective can colour by date too; DaisyDisk’s site does not list a view by age.

Deleting is deliberate

Klarity moves things to the Trash only after you confirm, and anything over 1 GB requires a slide-to-confirm gesture rather than a click you can fire by accident. It never empties the Trash for you, which is also why trashing files from a full external drive frees nothing until you do. If you change your mind, the file is still there.

DaisyDisk goes the other way, on purpose: you drag items to a collector, press Delete, get five seconds to cancel, and then they are removed for good and not sent to the Trash. That frees the space at once. Which you prefer is a matter of temperament.

Hidden folders, included

Since 1.3.1, Klarity scans hidden folders such as ~/Library as part of a normal scan, and Library has its own shortcut in the sidebar. That matters because System Data often hides in exactly those places. Earlier versions skipped them, and this page said so before anyone bought; that limitation is gone.

Who should stay with DaisyDisk

Plenty of people, genuinely:

  • If you like the sunburst. Some people read the ring instantly and find rectangles fiddly. Visual preference is not a bug to be argued with, and if the sunburst is how your head works, switching would make you slower.
  • If you already own it. You have paid; it works; three dollars is not a reason to relearn a tool.
  • If you rely on something Klarity does not have. Klarity is a young app, on version 1.3.2. A fifteen-year-old app has features and edge-case handling mine has not caught up with, and if your workflow depends on one of them, staying put is the right call.

What else is in Klarity

It needs macOS 14.6 or later, is sandboxed and asks for disk access once per volume through Apple’s own folder picker (1.3’s answer to the permissions bug in Macworld’s Klarity Disk review), has six colour themes, and since 1.2 meets WCAG 2.1 AA contrast and works with VoiceOver. Questions go to the support page, which has my email on it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best alternative to DaisyDisk?

It depends on what you want to change. For a free app, GrandPerspective draws a treemap and OmniDiskSweeper gives a plain list, largest first. For a treemap that also colours files by age, lists large files and shows memory use, Klarity is $6.99 once. If you like DaisyDisk’s rings and only wondered about the price, there is little to gain: DaisyDisk is $9.99 once.

Is there a free alternative to DaisyDisk?

Yes. GrandPerspective is free from its own site and open source. OmniDiskSweeper is free from The Omni Group. macOS has a Storage view in System Settings that lists your largest files with nothing to install. DaisyDisk also has a free trial on its own site, with deleting locked.

Is DaisyDisk a subscription?

No. DaisyDisk is $9.99 once, from its own site or the Mac App Store. Its developer says a future major upgrade may be a paid one, and that it would not cancel a licence you already have.

Is Klarity a one-time purchase?

Yes. Klarity is $6.99 once on the Mac App Store. There is no subscription, no account to create, and no in-app purchase to unlock features.

Does Klarity send any data off my Mac?

No. Klarity has zero network access — the sandbox it ships with forbids both incoming and outgoing connections, so it cannot talk to a server even if it wanted to. There are no accounts and no analytics.

Can I move files to the Trash from Klarity?

Yes, after you confirm. Klarity moves items to the Trash only once you approve it, and anything over 1 GB needs a deliberate slide-to-confirm. Klarity never empties the Trash, so you can always put something back.
